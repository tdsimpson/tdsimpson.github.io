<Section
  id="challenge"
  eyebrow="2. The need for SettleKit"
  heading="Setting up a life in the U.S. is a maze"
>

Being a newcomer in the U.S. means facing a maze of bureaucratic steps that are often hard to navigate by yourself (e.g., opening a bank account, qualifying for an apartment and getting health insurance). Each of these has its own rules and depends on your specific situation.

We’ve conducted over 50 user interviews, asking people what they struggled with most during their move to the U.S. Most people struggle to know where to start, where to get accurate information, and how to choose between options they’ve never had to think about before. Many of them currently get outdated, irrelevant or confusing information from friends, online threads or WhatsApp groups, where some people try to take advantage of them. Hiring a professional isn’t always an option, since it can be expensive.

Healthcare and housing were two of the biggest struggles. With healthcare, several people had gone months without coverage, one of them for seven months, because they were overwhelmed and didn’t know what to choose. With housing, many people get confused about the order of things. To apply for a Social Security number you need a mailing address, but to rent an apartment you often need a Social Security number. People end up signing leases on apartments they’ve never seen, or can’t work out why they keep getting turned down.

<Section
  id="problem"
  eyebrow="3. The problem"
  heading="A powerful engine with a confusing experience"
>

SettleKit's underlying personalization is very powerful, combining your country of origin, state and visa type into tens of thousands of starting paths, which then diverge based on what you’ve already done. When I joined, though, the experience didn’t do it justice. A lot of it felt incomplete, and many people weren’t using it:

- There were only about five topics (called “blocks” in the app)
- The landing page wasn’t clear about what SettleKit offers or how it would help
- The dashboard surfaced one recommended action at a time, driven by a short set of unexplained questions
- People were dropped straight into questions with no sense of *why* they were answering them or *where* the journey was headed
- The wording often didn’t feel relevant to people’s situations
- The information was there, but the steps weren’t packaged in a way that was easy to take in, so people were still overwhelmed

I own the user experience and the words, working closely with the founder, a small team of engineers, and sales and marketing.

- Write spec docs that justify each decision with pros, cons, and alternatives

<Section
  id="guides"
  eyebrow="5. The core reframe"
  heading="From a rigid checklist to explanatory guides"
>

When we interviewed users and looked at how they moved through the product, the same pattern kept coming up. People were given a list of steps but had no real sense of why each one mattered or how it connected to the next. Without the “why,” they had little motivation to start and no mental model of how the pieces fit together. Many left topics half-finished, because they were confused and overwhelmed.

The big issue I noticed was the format itself. Everything in SettleKit had been forced into strict, one-to-one steps that users marked “done.” On paper, that would seem like the right approach, where it’s concrete and concise. It worked for more familiar and straightforward actions like getting a driver’s license, but not as well for anything that was more nuanced and complex like healthcare in the U.S. Asking someone to check off “understand how U.S. healthcare works” left them wondering: *Did I finish that? Does “done” mean I understand it, or just that I read it?*

So I reframed the product’s approach from a checklist to explanatory guides. Each guide leads with context (why this matters, what’s at stake, and what “done” actually looks like) before getting into the actions. And because not everything is a step to check off, guides became resources people can return to as their situation changes, rather than a checklist to click through.

For example, here’s one step from our guide to getting through US immigration, first as it used to look and then as it looks now. This version is for someone on an H-1B visa, and people on other visas see the documents that apply to them.

<Section
  id="writing"
  eyebrow="6. The writing system"
  heading="Writing the rules behind every personalized path"
>

SettleKit generates guidance for each person’s situation, so there was no way to write every path by hand. Instead, I authored the source documents, guardrails and examples our AI-generated guidance is built from. They set how each guide is structured, what it needs to cover and how it should sound, so every path stays accurate and sounds like SettleKit. Because these guidelines run at build time, they shape every path generated for each person’s situation, not just a single screen.

The rules are written for the audience: immigrants from many different backgrounds, often reading in their second or third language and dealing with a lot at once. They cover every layer of the product, from the steps inside each guide to the short descriptions and outcomes people see before they start one.

### Example rules for the sidebar preview

The sidebar is what people see before they commit to starting a topic. Each rule comes with an example of what to avoid and what to write instead, so both people and the AI can apply it the same way.

The outcome guidelines end with a short checklist anyone can run before publishing, such as whether each point is under eight words and whether a non-native speaker would read it the way it’s intended. With these guidelines in place, producing a new topic went from weeks to days. Drafts came out much closer to what we wanted and were accurate from the start, so they needed far less revision. The same documents now power SettleKit’s in-app chat, which answers with each person’s situation in mind.

</Section>

<Section
  id="wayfinding"
  eyebrow="7. Progressive disclosure"
  heading="Revealing information in stages"
>

I redesigned the end-to-end flow around progressive disclosure. People shouldn’t be dropped somewhere without the context they need, but they also shouldn’t be buried in information before they need it. The goal is to show the right amount at each stage, so they always know enough to take the next step.

Deciding how much to show, and how much control to give, was one of the hardest parts. Some people want the tool to suggest what’s next, while others want more control, like marking things as done or snoozing them for later. We chose to start with more options and more explanation, because the system is confusing enough that over-explaining is safer than under-explaining. Since then, we’ve looked at what people actually use, along with conversations with them, to decide what to simplify.

### Progressive disclosure in onboarding

Onboarding taught us that timing matters as much as the message. In the first version, people answered a few basic questions and then went straight to the dashboard, where a series of pop-ups explained how the tool worked. Over 80% of people closed them without reading, since pop-ups are something we’ve all learned to dismiss, and these felt like noise on top of what people actually wanted to use.

The information was still worth getting across, so we moved it into the onboarding itself. Rather than describing the tool in general terms, the onboarding now asks a few more detailed questions, including what people have already done, and uses their answers to build a roadmap before they ever see the dashboard. Seeing their own path first gives people a sense of where they are and what’s left, so when they land on the dashboard they understand what it’s for and are ready to start working through their topics. Before the change, about 60% of people rushed through onboarding, looked at a couple of topics and then left, which suggested they didn’t really understand the tool or feel invested in it. With the new onboarding, that dropped to about 40%.

<Section
  id="handoffs"
  eyebrow="8. Provider handoffs"
  heading="Handoffs to commercial providers"
>

Most of what SettleKit helps with doesn’t actually happen in SettleKit. Opening a bank account, getting insurance or applying for a Social Security number all happen somewhere else, so a big part of guiding people is getting them to the right place with the right information. Some of those handoffs are straightforward. For a Social Security number there’s one government agency and one process, so we explain what to expect and send people there.

<Pull lead="The way I describe it to the team:">We act like a parent, holding their hand through everything. Then we tell them to go spend the afternoon with a stranger. So we have to set them up for it.</Pull>

Commercial providers are harder, because the person has to make a choice. As a newcomer you often don’t know what’s out there, which options are open to you without a U.S. credit history, or what you’d need to do to qualify for the ones that aren’t. That’s usually when people go looking for advice, so I designed a comparison experience that gives them the full lay of the land in one place. Instead of simple filters, people mark how much each factor matters to them, from “don’t care” to “dealbreaker,” and the banks are ranked to match. Someone without an SSN yet can rule out every bank that requires one, while someone else might care most about monthly fees or a joining bonus.

<Figure

Every bank has the same standardized details page covering eligibility, benefits and things to watch out for, so people can compare options fairly instead of reading each company’s sales page.

That details page also prepares people for the handoff itself. Before they choose a bank and leave SettleKit, it explains how the process works, what they’ll need to apply and what to watch out for, like a delay in getting routing numbers after signing up from abroad. That way people show up prepared instead of figuring it out on the spot. The whole handoff experience also helped us win our first bank partnerships.

<Section id="impact" eyebrow="9. Impact" heading="Results so far">

Two changes made the clearest difference. Moving to guides raised the share of started topics people finished by about 50%, and the new onboarding cut the share of people who left after a quick look from about 60% to 40%. People also became more deliberate about what they started, with 90% previewing more than one topic in their first session before choosing. Over the same period, SettleKit grew from fewer than 10 weekly users to around 200, and the principles I pushed for, like progressive disclosure and the writing guidelines, became how the team builds new features.

<Section
  id="learned"
  eyebrow="10. What I learned"
  heading="Lessons from building SettleKit"
>

- **People rarely start from zero.** Early on, we built SettleKit as if it were a tool people found at the beginning of their journey. In reality, most had already gotten help from a lawyer or a friend, done things out of order, or made mistakes (like signing a long-term lease before arriving). Now, during our onboarding, we make sure to capture what people have already done. The tool helps them check whether it was done right and helps them recover if it wasn’t.
- **We are no longer just writing for humans.** Since SettleKit starts from tens of thousands of different paths, we can’t write guides for everyone. Instead, we write rules and guidelines for our tools to follow. Those rules and examples can quietly contradict each other, and a rule that looks clear on paper may still not work until you reword it. You have to spell out edge cases, say which rules win when they conflict, and check that every new piece fits what’s already there.
- **We need to know the process better than our partners’ docs.** Since SettleKit is a guidance tool instead of the place where actions are done, our handoffs to partners are so important. In many cases, those partner guides go out of date, confuse people, or link to broken pages, and that’s exactly where people get stuck and give up. We have to know each process ourselves and spot when their docs aren’t good enough. When they aren’t, we point out the pitfalls and anything that’s out of date or broken, so people know what to watch out for before they run into it.

</Section>
