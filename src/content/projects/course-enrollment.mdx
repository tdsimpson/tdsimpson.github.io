<Section
  id="problem"
  eyebrow="2. The problem"
  heading="Missing the context students need"
>

Oracle’s enrollment platform exposes the raw logic of its database, but it’s missing a layer of context that matches how students actually search, plan, and decide. The core enrollment mechanics are there, but little else is done to help students make sense of a course before they commit. That means they have to fill in the gaps themselves during one of the most stressful parts of the academic year.

<Section id="research" eyebrow="3. Research" heading="What students told me">

I interviewed six people, five with direct experience using Oracle’s enrollment software. The questions covered their most recent registration experience, where they go for requirements and course info, their biggest frustrations, and what external tools they lean on to get through the process.

Almost everyone described the same pattern. To finish enrollment, they had to leave the platform and piece things together themselves:

- Opening a program requirements flowchart on a separate site
- Leaving the tool to look up what a course actually is, since there’s no real way to browse or explore courses inside enrollment
- Checking a personal calendar or third-party schedule builder in another tab for conflicts

<Section
  id="feedback"
  eyebrow="8. Expert feedback"
  heading="Feedback from Amazon and EA"
>

I presented the prototype to UX designers from Amazon and EA. The overall response was strong, with their main suggestion being to pre-add required courses onto a student’s schedule so they wouldn’t have to add those themselves.

I personally pushed back on that because:

- Required courses often have several sections, so auto-adding one means guessing which lecture or tutorial someone wants.
- It assumes they want that course this term. Plenty of students run a lighter load, spread requirements across semesters, or skip a course on purpose.

A softer suggestion or default could be worth exploring later, but forcing those courses onto the schedule felt like the wrong default to me.

</Section>

<Section
  id="learned"
  eyebrow="9. What I learned"
  heading="Lessons from the enrollment redesign"
>

### What I’d do differently

- Early interviews were harder than they needed to be. I tried to facilitate and take notes at the same time, and I missed follow-ups. Once I started recording sessions, I could stay in the conversation and pull the detail afterward.
- Mid-project I kept rebuilding the same UI pieces because I hadn’t set up reusable components. A light design system earlier would have saved a lot of time.
- On accessibility, I initially treated hover tooltips as enough. Making key ones clickable mattered for anyone not using a mouse.

### Where I’d take it next

- Interview a wider and more varied audience.
- Build a more defined, documented design system.
- Test the prototype with more students and iterate on the findings.
- Extend it to the school’s administrative side.
- Revisit calendar course-editing (the tooltip modal) and add an availability-icon legend.

</Section>
